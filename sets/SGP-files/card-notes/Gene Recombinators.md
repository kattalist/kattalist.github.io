## The Son And The Moon
### A *Stirrings in Goldport* Story

The cabin rattled. Like shrapnel, rain pelted the window in sheets, catching the roaring winds of the late afternoon storm and coming in sideways. Only a dull roar penetrated the hull, but the pane stung like ice. The Evelline groaned, resisting the gale’s compulsion to drift further skyward, and the turbulence cast a sickly fog of unease into the smokey air inside.
Leonard rapped his fingers against the frigid glass, trying to look past the roiling grey curtains to the skyline of Goldport coming up below. He and his colleagues were what felt like five hours into their three-hour trip from Frossenhold. He knew it was at least three from his pocket watch, but he had forced it away to curb obsession. His solution was to focus on the raindrops, watching them impact the glass, then snake down and pool at the bottom of the frame. He hated how they shook with such eclectic chaos, how they whipped back and forth as the cabin tossed and turned. He hated how air travel reduced him to a pawn, a tchotchke at the mercy of the winds. Science had brought all the secrets of the world to humanity’s fingertips, and here they were, fragile as dandelion seeds.
He shook off the stewardess as she tiptoed down the aisle, trying her best to keep a tray of drinks upright. Everything in the Evelline’s hold tasted like stale piss, so Leonard had arranged for new liquors to be stocked when the Institute flew it from Goldport. By now, they were souring as well.
Stelver sat opposite him, engrossed in his newspaper. The fedora he wore bowed over his forehead, obscuring his eyes completely. Between him and Leonard billowed a think chimney of smoke from his pipe, and the slivers of light peering from outside the cabin cast a spectral aura upon him.
“Look at this here.” Stelver turned the paper to Leonard. “The Scarlet Promise. Isn’t that the show you like?”
“Surprised you remembered.”
“Well, it’ll be running at the LaCloff Theatre starting next week.”
“Huh.” Leonard gave a stiff nod. “Who’s playing Meryl in that?”
Stelver frowned, leaning forward towards the page. “Phoebe Wellings.”
“That’s what I was worried about.” Leonard slumped against the plush of his chair. “She doesn’t have the range. Vocally or as an actor. I could name at least three other actresses who would fit the part better right now.”
“I have no opinion on this.”
“I won’t say the real reason I think she got cast, but I will say that Minister Wellings and Minister LaCloff are very chummy.”
“You’re one to talk about nepotism, Mr. Clanston,” Stelver chuckled.
Leonard scoffed. “You can take that up with him for buying the Institute. I had my accolades in order before he decided to take over.”
“Maybe I will.” Stelver peered out the window. “And maybe if we land soon I won’t tell him he needs to find a new pilot.”
Silence fell. Stelver returned to his reading, Leonard to his wrenching ruminations and finger percussion. In a matter of minutes, the sky parted, and the Evelline mercifully entered descent. Goldport was a miniature model, a decorative arrangement of ornate blocks glowing in the evening light. Floating from it were motes, airships cast free from their hangars sent to join them in their pilgrimage across the sky. Below it all, schooners and liners alike painted pearly streaks across the sparkling cerulean sea. Home. A respite from the boreal grey of Frossenhold.
Stelver broke the silence, catching Leonard in his stupor. “What do you think your father will make of this?”
This. The scrawlings in his breastpocket started to burn a hole in his chest. “We can’t know for certain.”
“But he will trust us.”
“He has to.” His heart, normally measured and gentle, thundered in his ears. He peered out the window. where the faintest, imperceptible vignette tainted the warm, cream-coloured sky. “We have to make him.”

Throughout Leonard’s life, his father had told him that Clanston Manor was founded anywhere from fifty to five hundred years ago. While he hadn’t perused the annals of its occupancy, he knew countless generations of Clanstons before him had enjoyed its grounds; even amongst the outer-city rows of manor homes, his father’s stead stood as one of the largest and most brilliant. 
To avoid too much fuss, he and Stelver had made the trip from the hangar alone. The telegram from there had informed his father of the nature of the meeting, of the importance that its contents should be kept secret. Even now, as he walked past the grand white foundation and up the many manor steps, his fingers traced the leather binding of his journal. He was interrupted by Stelver near the top step, who pointed skyward.
“Look.” The sun was setting, and the pale moon, no bigger than a dime, had crested the tops of the old-growth conifers around the property. There it loomed over Calloran, a sentinel of rock, as it always had and would. And then… it shifted. Not in any way that could be explained, at least not without a telescope, but almost as if its craters and features had been smeared across its surface with a paintbrush. Only for a minute did they stay this way, before tessellating back to their original position. “You saw that?” he asked.
“Yeah. That was it again, yes?”
“Looked like it. That weird phasing thing.” He pressed the doorbell, and a muffled chime rang inside. “You have the drawings handy?”
Leonard nodded as the doors creaked and a butler in full tuxedo and white intricate gloves pulled them apart. Approaching the pair with a wide smile was Leonard’s father, tall and lanky as he was but with the hair and wrinkles to match his advanced years. His suit was pristine black, and the heels of his shoes clattered loudly against the foyer’s marble floor.
“Son. Dr. Stelver. A pleasure to see you both.”
“Father.” Leonard bowed his head and replied with a firm handshake.
“Was the Evelline all right for you both?”
“If you were sober, maybe.”
“Apologies to you, then,” he said to Stelver, a joking smile crinkling the creases of his eyes. He made a quick motion with his hand, and the butler left briskly. “I received your telegram.”
“Yes. The study would be best.”
“Indeed.” Minister Clanston led the pair up the foyer’s grand staircase, ambling with the vigour of a man twenty years his junior, to a dark-walled room packed tightly with deep wood bookshelves and oil portraits around a spotless rectangular desk. The second-floor window, framed in interwoven marble and gold, looked over the escarpment and onto the skyline of Goldport proper. He closed the door behind them and made himself comfortable in the room’s only chair. “How can I help you both?”
“We…” Leonard stammered, reaching for his breastpocket and coming up empty. What he was attempting to describe was too bizarre for even the wildest esoterics, and now here he was looking for support from his father. Eventually, his clawed grip grasped hold of a sheet of paper, nearly ripping it in the struggle to wrest his notes from his pocket. “We think something has changed with the Moon.”
His father looked bewildered. He turned to Stelver, as if to say you agree with this? “What’s changed?”
A couple deep breaths. In, out. Look at the notes. “While representatives of the Institute were in Frossenhold for the symposium, Dr. Canteau noticed a strange visual anomaly on the surface of the moon. It was difficult to describe, but he likened it to colours being swirled around on a palette. Naturally, we thought he was just seeing things, but he was as adamant as I had ever seen him. Up and down, he insisted that something was really wrong. Then Dr. Robney took a look, and she noticed the same thing. Same exact description, same insistence that she wasn’t losing her mind.
“We took our case to the city observatory to take a better look at things. We know Tomas well, so he was able to get us in past open hours. Even past the curfew for university researchers and the like. Anyway, we took a look, and we all confirmed it. This is what we saw.” He passed his father the unfolded notes, onto which he had sketched multiple stills of the contorting face of the moon.
“We’re bringing this to your attention, Minister,” added Stelver, “because we believe the Clanston Institute should allocate funds and manpower to investigate this anomaly specifically. We need to understand what it is, if it poses a threat to us, and if its eventual reveal will pose a threat to the general population.”
“Ontological shock,” his father mumbled. “If people believe the Moon is trying to speak to them… their view of the world will be shattered. They’ll become husks of themselves. Or worse.” His expression steeled. “Has this happened again?”
“Moments before we arrived. Out front.”
“And it appeared in the same way?”
“Virtually so.”
He pushed his chin into his upturned palm, thinking for a few moments. “This is deeply concerning, son. I agree. I have no issue reallocating the Institute’s research to prioritize this. And as a matter of fact, while you were gone, something happened which you might find interesting.” He slid open a desk drawer and passed his own stack of parchment to Leonard.
The sheets contained mind-numbing tables and bullet-point notes, but nestled just under the first was a photograph of a creature unlike anything Leonard had seen before. It was squid-like at the base, even though its lower appendages were sharply bent like legs of a spider, but its top half was a grotesque, amorphous hack of flesh. Other outstretched bits could have been arms, or pincers, but the thing’s entire form was unnerving to gaze upon or to describe. It was surrounded by instruments and scientists, rolled on its side with all the petrifying uncaniness of a dead fish.
“What is it?” Leonard asked.
“We don’t know. While you were gone, constables and medics were responding to an incident reported on the highways. A family had been travelling and they had been attacked, and the young man who rang it in was the only one who got away. They got to the site of the incident and found a woman and two children torn to shreds. Nearby was this thing, just writhing on the ground and close to death.
“In any case, a couple scientists who stayed behind overheard the case, took the body into the lab, did the necropsy, sequencing, et cetera.” He took a deep breath. “It’s not from this world. Its anatomy doesn’t resemble any other living thing. We’ve come no closer to understanding what it is or how it works. We know it was once alive, and that’s it.”
Leonard continued to rifle through the papers. Vitals, experiment logs, crude anatomical diagrams. All of it arriving at his father’s conclusion. “You think these are somehow connected?”
“I think it would be strange for these two supernatural events to occur at the same time otherwise.” He stood. “I’ll be speaking to the Duchess tomorrow. We have the research capacity but no militia to protect our investigators. If we want to understand this, we need more. 
“That’s why I’d like to arrange for a secret ‘task force’ to be assembled. It would combine the strongest members of our research corps with capable fighting forces and constables. Under the Institute’s tutelage, there is much we can do in order to understand what this thing was, and what it has to do with what you saw.” The moon had risen further, and was now beautifully centred in the study window. “And if this phenomenon is visible to the naked eye, we must act fast.”
Leonard and Stelver exchanged a nervous glance. “What should we do?”
“Tomorrow, speak with Dr. Lantha Desaerin. He led the investigations on our end. I’ll be speaking to him soon as well to have a sizable role in this new task force, should it come to fruition.” He read the looks he was receiving well. “You two also will, of course.”
“I’ll bring this to Desaerin, then.” Leonard tucked the other papers into his jacket. Though his depictions had weighed on him like a second heartbeat that warred against his own, they now interwove with each other and himself in perfect syzygy. The image of the thing had burned a hole in his mind’s eye. With every blink he saw its tentacles, its mass, its fractal maw more clearly. 
A beast beyond Calloran’s domain. A messenger from another world.
